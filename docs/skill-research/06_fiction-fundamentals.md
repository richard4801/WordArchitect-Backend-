# 6. Fiction fundamentals for a story this size

This file is the machinery under the romance: why one event follows another, why a reader believes it, and how a long book keeps its threads and people moving. All of it comes back to one idea, so I'll put it first. **The next thing that happens should come out of what somebody wants.** The diagnosis found the same on the page. When this book goes stiff, the next event came from the moon count or a piece of furniture. When it's alive, it came from a person.

I read every page listed at the end. Where a famous line turned out to be misquoted, I've said so. Reviews are paraphrased, with the book named.

---

## 1. Desire: somebody in the scene wants something from somebody

Kurt Vonnegut's third rule of fiction, from his introduction to *Bagombo Snuff Box*, is that every character should want something, even if the want is as small as a drink of water. Jim Butcher makes it exact in his posts on scene structure: a scene is the stretch where the viewpoint character goes after a goal that is clear, looks reachable, is specific, and matters to her. "Survive the night" isn't a scene goal. "Get Nadia out of that room before he turns" is.

A want also needs a target. Jeanne Cavelos of the Odyssey workshop has an example worth pinning above the desk. Joe suggests bowling, a film, dinner, and Jane turns down each one. Joe is active, Jane is reactive, and readers side with Joe because at least he's trying. Refusing isn't wanting. The diagnosis counted Ronan's lines to Maren from Ch 55 to 70: about forty-five, more than twenty-five of them bare orders, which she mostly refuses. That's Joe and Jane for sixteen chapters, except this Joe only gives orders, so nobody is trying.

The best example of desire driving a plot is in this book. Maren wants him to hurt, so she stops eating (Ch 50). Her hunger lands in his gut in another woman's bed (Ch 58). He works out that it's aimed at him (Ch 63), forces food on her in the car (Ch 64), and her full belly gives him his first quiet in days (Ch 65). Five chapters, no calendar, no prop, and every link is someone wanting something from one person. Set it beside the bar, carried from chapter to chapter since Ch 64. Nobody wants anything from a bar, so the chapters built on it feel like furniture.

Readers notice. On Goodreads, a reviewer of Jessica Hall's *Alpha's Regret*, a platform-born werewolf hit, dismissed its heroine as weak for simply giving in to the hero, and a reviewer of Umme Kulsum's *The Alpha's Bride* complained of a heroine who flails and cries. The warmest reviews of Asha Nyr's dark captive romance *The Mistake and the Lycan King* praise a heroine who gets through on wits. That one matters most for us, because Ragna, like Maren, is in a cage she can't walk out of. In a captive book, agency doesn't mean escaping. It means choosing inside the cage and paying for the choice.

One honest disagreement. Donald Maass, at Writer Unboxed, argues that a passive protagonist can carry a book if she's seeking rather than suffering, helpless but not hopeless. He's right, and the laundry chapters (Ch 22 and 23) prove it: Maren only endures, and it's the best writing in the book. But inward seeking buys a few chapters, not a middle. Past Ch 70, she has to start seeking out loud.

## 2. Cause and effect: "but" and "therefore", never "and then"

Trey Parker and Matt Stone told a writing class at NYU that between every beat of an outline there should be "either the word 'therefore' or 'but.'" If the honest word between two beats is "and then," you have a list, not a story. (Speakola's transcript dates the class to 6 March 2014; several write-ups say 2011.) Aristotle got there first. In the *Poetics* he wrote that "it makes all the difference whether any given event is a case of propter hoc or post hoc," meaning whether it happens because of the last thing or merely after it, and that "of all plots and actions the episodic are the worst." Emma Coats's list of Pixar story rules turns it into a fill-in-the-blank: once upon a time, every day, one day, because of that, because of that, until finally.

Try the test on the author's dead plan: Ch 71 The Press, Ch 72 The Beast, Ch 73 Rhett, Ch 74 the fight. The only thing you can put between those four nouns is "and then." The author hated what the plan said and how it was written, and those were one fault. A list of events reads like a list because it is one. A plan written in "so" and "but" reads like a story because it is one. A habit worth keeping: when an outline sentence starts with "Then," ask what caused the thing. If you can't say, the beat isn't caused yet, and good prose around it won't hide that.

Coincidence is the other half. Pixar's rule 19 says a coincidence that drops characters into trouble is fine and one that gets them out is cheating. Aristotle adds that even chance hits harder when it looks designed: the statue of Mitys at Argos fell on Mitys's murderer while he watched a festival. A Goodreads reviewer of *The Mistake and the Lycan King* named the failure exactly: outside forces only started moving when the author wanted more drama. That's what a moon count does when it supplies events. It's weather, and readers can tell weather from want.

Ch 70 shows the right way. Brida stops Maren's hand on the bolt. That isn't a lucky rescue, because Maren sent Brida below earlier and Brida didn't go, and her bare feet have been on the page beside Maren's without comment. A choice made earlier caused the rescue. The reader gets it a beat after it happens, and that click is what cause and effect feels like from the inside.

## 3. Set-up and payoff

Almost everyone quotes a version of Chekhov's gun that Chekhov may not have written. His letter to Aleksandr Lazarev of 1 November 1889 says not to put a loaded rifle on stage if nobody means to fire it. The pistol on the wall in act one comes from Ilia Gurlyand's memoir of 1904. The Nabokov Society's notes keep them apart. The wording differs; the idea doesn't.

Brandon Sanderson's version suits a long serial better: promise, progress, payoff. The promise is what you've told the reader to expect, progress is the felt movement toward it, and the payoff is an answer that surprises and still satisfies. His sharpest claim, in his 2025 BYU plot lecture, is that readers mostly drop a book because they stopped seeing signs of progress. That's this book's mid-run risk. Thirty-six open threads are thirty-six promises, and the diagnosis counts five discoveries in seventy chapters, none of which exposed a villain.

Two payoffs worth studying. In the first chapter of *Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone*, Hagrid mentions borrowing the motorbike from Sirius Black, who walks on in book three. In *Great Expectations*, which Dickens ran in weekly parts in *All the Year Round* from 1 December 1860, the convict Magwitch terrifies Pip in the first chapter and doesn't return properly until Chapter 39, in the part dated 11 May 1861. Weekly readers had five months to half forget him, which is why the return works. Sanderson says the same of Gandalf's promise in *The Two Towers* to come back at dawn on the fifth day: the battle buries it, so it pays like a rescue instead of a schedule. A payoff lands best when the reader has half forgotten the set-up and knows it the moment it fires.

This book has guns on the wall nobody has touched. In Ch 5 Ronan remembers "her soft hands stroking my fur in sleep." Her hands now have numb tips, lifted nails and a dead white patch from the lye, and nobody has put the two side by side. In Ch 17 he feels under his jaw "for a bite scar that had never been there" and thinks a mate's bite would explain all of it, and nobody has mentioned a bite since. The bolt on the King's door is on the outside, where anyone can draw it. In Ch 56 he tells her that next time she won't kneel she should say so and not use his name. Every one of those is loaded, and every one points at the couple.

There's a trap here, and Sanderson's First Law of magic names it: an author's ability to solve a conflict with magic is directly proportional to how well the reader understands that magic. Apply it to this book's two powers. The bond is hard magic. The reader knows its rules: feeling travels both ways, his side is clear and hers faint, dull pain doesn't cross and sharp pain does, she can't feel his hunger. So the bond is allowed to settle things, the way her hunger settled what happened with Nadia. The prophecy is soft magic, stated once in Ilsa's head in Ch 10. By Sanderson's law it can make problems (Rhett and Ilsa fear it) but can't solve one. A prophecy that heals the King is magic doing a choice's job, which the rulebook already marks as slop.

## 4. Escalation

Escalation doesn't mean more. It means harder to undo. Shawn Coyne's Story Grid calls the steps progressive complications: each ties back to what started the trouble, each raises the stakes, and together they use up the hero's usual tactics until she's forced into a real choice. Story Grid splits turning points into active ones, where somebody's action wrecks the plan, and revelatory ones, where new information does. Mix them. Five revelations in a row is a mystery novel; five fights in a row is an action film.

Vonnegut's sixth rule is to be a sadist: make awful things happen to your sweet lead so we see what she's made of. That's a direction, not a call for more cruelty. Aim the awful thing at what she can't stand. For Maren that's being used and put back: the laundry, and the breeder rule that takes the son and sends the mother back to the vats.

The enemy of escalation is repetition. Robert McKee calls it the law of diminishing returns: as readers transcribe *Story*, a repeated emotional beat lands at half strength the second time and can flip to its opposite the third. I could only check that through quote collections, so treat it as a rule of thumb. The same collections have him exempting sex, which he says repeats well. Careful: sex can repeat, but a sex scene that leaves the two people where it found them is the same beat twice. The diagnosis has the evidence: Ch 57 to 59 hit "she's hungry, so he can't" three times, the third mirror of Ilsa's intake (Ch 43 to 48) makes the reader pity Ilsa, and the fat insults stopped landing around Ch 30.

The best-known stall in long fantasy is Robert Jordan's *Crossroads of Twilight*, book ten of *The Wheel of Time*. A 2003 review at The Core Dump said essentially nothing happens and asked for an editor to cut the pointless subplots. Brandon Sanderson, who finished the series, defends it: Jordan showed the same stretch of time from viewpoint after viewpoint, all circling an offstage event, the Cleansing, that nobody can react to because nobody knows what happened. He also admits it sits around one and a half stars on Amazon. Both are right: the craft is real, and readers felt no escalation because nobody could act on the big event. That's the danger of a moon night the whole palace hears and nobody acts on.

Butcher's fix for a sagging middle is worth stealing. He plans a "Big Middle": one large event, set up well ahead, whose fallout drives the book into its last act. In *Dead Beat* it's the zombie attack on Harry's apartment. This book has been building its Big Middle since Ch 17, and it's the moon night. Ch 71 has to deliver it, and Ch 72 to 75 have to run on what it breaks.

## 5. "Yes, but" and "No, and"

Butcher's scene posts give four answers to the question every scene asks: does she get what she went in for? A plain yes he calls a no-no, because it ends the drama. A plain no he allows with care, since it can halt the story. Yes-but, where she gets it at a cost that comes due later, is better. No-and-furthermore, where she doesn't and things get worse, is his favourite. Then comes the sequel: she reacts, thinks, weighs where each option leads, and chooses, and the choice becomes the next scene's goal. That last step is the engine. A heroine who reacts and never chooses is reactive however much she shouts.

The book has done both answers well. Ch 49 is yes-but. Maren shouts "Stop!" at the whipping, and the King obeys her in front of the yard ("Nadia's mouth went tight as the King obeyed me"). She gets it. But he resents obeying a laundry girl in public, and when Maren hears Nadia is going to his bed she reads it as his punishment for the yard. It isn't (Rhett chose Nadia in Ch 47), and that wrong reading drives her into the hunger strike. Ch 66 is no-and. She wants him, she smells Nadia on him and shoves him off, and now she believes something false that his pride won't correct. Ch 70 is no-and too. She reaches for the bolt to let Nadia out, Brida stops her, and on top of that the King says he never took the bar, so there's a thief in the wing.

Where the book skimps is the choice. Maren hides the missing bar from Halvard in Ch 68 with nothing on the page behind it, which is why it reads as stupid instead of bold. You don't need a paragraph of inner explaining to fix that (the author's voice rules forbid one). You need one concrete thing she notices or wants that makes the choice hers.

## 6. Surprise that feels inevitable

Aristotle wanted the events of a tragedy to "come on us by surprise" and also to "follow as cause and effect." The strongest moment, he said, is a recognition (a change from ignorance to knowledge) that arrives with a reversal, like the messenger in *Oedipus* who comes to reassure and, by revealing who Oedipus is, does the opposite. Forster's test for a round character is the same idea in a person: is she "capable of surprising in a convincing way"? Sanderson asks his payoffs to be unexpected and inevitable at once.

Here's how the genre's most borrowed-from romance does it. In *Pride and Prejudice*, Wickham tells Elizabeth his sad history the first evening they talk, and she believes him because she already dislikes Darcy. Twenty chapters later Darcy's letter gives her the truth. The surprise doesn't come from new facts. It comes from old ones she filed wrong. Rereading, she is "struck with the impropriety of such communications to a stranger, and wondered it had escaped her before." Then: "Till this moment, I never knew myself." The reader filed the same clues the same wrong way, so both recognitions happen in one moment. That's the trick. The surprise has to live in a fact the reader already holds.

*Great Expectations* does it to a whole plot. Pip and the reader assume Miss Havisham is his benefactor because Pip wants it to be her. When Magwitch walks back in, everything Pip built on that guess turns over, and every clue was on the page. Then Dickens wires the cast into the same secret: Estella is the daughter of Magwitch and Molly, Jaggers's housekeeper, and the man who deserted Miss Havisham is Magwitch's old partner, Compeyson. One reveal re-lights five people.

The failure is the reveal that only opens another question, which the rulebook calls a mystery answered with a mystery. Aristotle would call it recognition with no reversal: you learned something and nothing turned. Serial readers complain about the opposite failure too. A Goodreads reviewer of *Alpha's Regret* was fed up that nobody could put the obvious together. Dramatic irony is this book's best suspense, but left too long it turns into a reader shouting at a heroine who seems slow.

## 7. Running a large cast

The book has thirty-six open threads, six point-of-view characters and about fifteen named people with wants, in a story six days long. The old rules for a crowd hold.

Forster divides characters into flat and round. Flat ones, in their purest form, are "constructed round a single idea or quality." Mrs Micawber says she won't desert Mr Micawber, she doesn't, and that's her. Forster isn't sneering: flat characters are "easily recognized whenever they come in," and Dickens built crowded, living worlds out of them. Halvard's measured courtesy, Liv's tongue stud, the Head Cook's "Don't take it to heart," Sigrun's mildness: those are flat characters doing their job. A serial reader back after a week needs those tags. Keep them.

The danger is handing a flat character a round character's secret. Every dangling glance in the dossier (Sigrun's eyes going to Rhett, the Queen's to Halvard, the Head Cook's look) is a flat character asking to be round, and the book can't afford that many round people. Let most of the glances go.

The second rule is that the leads never leave. When George R. R. Martin split *A Feast for Crows* by character and place, the volume that came out first had no Tyrion, Daenerys or Jon. The Atlantic said the book badly missed them, and a Goodreads reviewer complained it was spread thin over too many minor viewpoints. In a romance, the couple is your Tyrion and Daenerys, and a secondary scene that doesn't land on Maren or Ronan is a *Feast for Crows* chapter. Platform readers agree. A Goodreads reviewer of *Alpha's Regret* said every side character getting a plot of their own was too much, and a roundup of low-star reviews of the reading apps collects complaints about filler that seems to exist to keep readers paying.

The third rule is Dickens's: wire the cast into a few shared secrets so one reveal pays several people. The fourth comes from Martin's own pain. In 2010 he was still writing on his blog about hacking at what he called the Meereenese knot, the job of getting a crowd of characters into one city in the right order, and he once tried to cut it by switching viewpoint. Moving many people to one place is slow. Bring people to the couple instead of sending the couple round the palace. The King's corridor, the onion store and the kitchen yard are already places where people end up face to face.

A last serial note. "Make 'em laugh, make 'em cry, make 'em wait" is pinned on Wilkie Collins by The Victorian Web and on Collins, Dickens or Charles Reade by other writers, and the wording shifts between "cry" and "weep." Whoever said it, the order matters. The wait is earned by the laughing and crying. On a coin-per-chapter platform, a wait with nothing to feel inside it is a wait the reader pays for.

## What this means for Chapters 71 to 75 of this book

**1. Open Ch 71 on a want aimed at a person.** At the last line of Ch 70 Maren wants Nadia out alive and Brida out of harm's way, and the King has just given Brida an order by name. A chapter that opens on two women dragging the press across the floor has no goal anyone can lose. Give Maren something specific she can fail to get, and answer it with yes-but or no-and. A clean yes ends the moon night too cheaply; a clean no is Ch 70 again.

**2. Pay the moon on the page, then let the fallout drive Ch 72 to 75.** The moon night is the Big Middle the book has planted since Ch 17. If Ch 72 to 75 could be told without it having happened, the run is wrong. A time skip is allowed, but the reader has to feel what caused the gap and what it cost.

**3. Let the bond settle things, never the prophecy.** An outcome that works by the bond's known rules is fair play. The prophecy can only frighten Rhett and Ilsa. Whether the bond and his memory survive the change hasn't been ruled, so flag either answer as the author's call.

**4. Fire two guns already on the wall, both aimed at the couple.** The strongest: the soft hands he remembers (Ch 5) against the ruined ones she has; the throat check for a bite (Ch 5, 17) on the next morning after a moon; the outside bolt anyone can draw (Ch 68 to 70); and "say so. Don't use my name" (Ch 56) for her next meeting with the Queen. Each should surprise a character while the reader recognizes it, the way Elizabeth rereads the letter.

**5. Make one reveal pay several threads.** Ilsa took the bar and the nightdress behind Rhett's back, and the page has shown how Rhett makes his sister pay (her keys taken for a season, meals with the laundry women, the lashes). Pull that thread and the bar, the nightdress and Ilsa-against-Rhett move together. Mind the guard: Ronan must never think Ilsa is Rhett's sister. The rulebook wants two threads closed, and wiring makes two cheap.

**6. End "he had Nadia" through somebody's want, not luck.** One sentence ends it, and only Ronan and Nadia (if she lives) can say it. Popp's line at Book Riot holds: a miscommunication fails when everything hangs on a fact nobody checks. Nadia has a want (never to go home "a girl nobody kept") and a fresh grudge ("Fuck you, Maren"), so if she tells it, she tells it to get something or to hurt. If Ronan tells it, it's his first admission out loud.

**7. Nothing at the same strength twice.** No fourth hunger-kills-the-sex beat, no more mirrors of Ilsa's intake, no fat insult without a price, no two chapters ending on the same kind of pull. If there's heat, they leave it somewhere new.

**8. Bring the cast to the couple.** Every secondary character in Ch 71 to 75 wants one thing that touches Maren or Ronan. No seventh viewpoint; if you leave Maren's head, go to Ronan's, where readers learn to love him. Keep Halvard, Sigrun and the Head Cook flat and let most glances go. If Skye surfaces after forty-six chapters away, the Tributes' wing is the natural door, and Brida stays out of that room.

**9. Turn orders into wants, on both sides.** In every shared chapter, give Ronan at least one question or admission instead of a command. He's done it twice ("Are you trying to punish me?", "I took nothing"), so it's in his voice. Give Maren one thing she wants for herself, said to him. Standing beside him, being wanted as herself: so far she has wanted those only in a dream (Ch 69).

**10. Write the outline the way you'd tell it.** The author threw out a plan for reading like a checklist, and this file says why: a list of nouns has nothing between them but "and then." Tell each chapter in prose, in order, joined by "so" and "but." Name the object, the line and the hand that moves. "Tension rises" means you haven't found the beat. Plain words, no padding, and enough opinion to sound like a person who can't wait to read this book. Read one chapter paragraph aloud before handing it in. If you can't say why each thing happened, the cause is missing, and no adjective will put it back.
