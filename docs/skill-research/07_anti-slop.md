# 7. Anti-slop: what reads as formula or machine-made, and what gives a book its own feel

"Slop" is a fair word for a real thing, and it helps to pin it down before trying to avoid it. Here's the definition this file works from: slop is what you get when every choice is the likeliest one. The likeliest word, the likeliest name, the likeliest next event, the likeliest way to end a chapter. Any one of those choices is fine on its own. A book made only of them reads as if nobody made it, and readers have learned to tell.

That matters for this tournament in a particular way. Thirty-two people are about to outline the same five chapters from the same files. If each takes the likeliest next step, the judges will read one outline thirty-two times with the names moved around. So this file covers two things: how to spot the likeliest choice at every level, from the word up to the plan, and what this book has that the likeliest choice can't reach.

I read every source listed at the end. Reddit, The Guardian and Ted Chiang's New Yorker essay wouldn't load, so Chiang comes here through a critic's summary, and I say so where he comes up. Reviews are quoted in short fragments.

---

## 1. What the machines over-use, and why that helps even if you never touch one

The hardest numbers come from "Antislop," a 2025 paper by Samuel Paech and colleagues, accepted at ICLR 2026. They had 67 language models write fiction from the same prompts and counted which words and phrases turned up far more often than in human writing. The name "Elara" appeared about 85,000 times more often in one model's stories than in human ones. "Flickered" made the over-used list for 98.5 percent of the models. The phrase the paper records as "voice barely whisper" (a voice barely above a whisper, small words stripped out) made the list for 68.7 percent, "took deep breath" for 44.8, and "eyes never leaving" for 29.9. The sentence shape "It's not X, it's Y" ran at 6.3 times the human rate in some models.

People wrote every one of those phrases first, thousands of times, which is how the models learned them. So the list is a map of the commonest human choice, and it helps anyone, with a model or without one, see where the average lives.

Wikipedia's editors keep a field guide to the same thing, "Signs of AI writing." Its words to watch include "pivotal," "testament," "underscores," "tapestry," "setting the stage for" and "evolving landscape." It flags sentences that end on a trailing "-ing" phrase pretending to analyse ("highlighting her resilience"), lists of three used to look thorough, the "not only... but also" family, paragraphs that close by restating their point, and headings in Title Case. It also gives the caveat to keep: these are signs, not proof. People wrote this way first.

Tuhin Chakrabarty, Philippe Laban and Chien-Sheng Wu took it further in "Can AI Writing Be Salvaged?" Professional writers edited 1,057 model-written paragraphs and sorted every fix into seven kinds: cliché, unnecessary exposition, purple prose, poor sentence structure, lack of specificity, awkward word choice, and tense slips. Unnecessary exposition made 18 percent of the edits and cliché 17. Circle the first. It means saying again what the scene already showed, and in an outline it turns into a character explaining her feelings.

The sources disagree on whose problem the average is. Ted Chiang argued in 2024 that art is many choices, and a model can only hand you a bland average of other people's choices (I'm going on Erik Hoel's summary). Hoel answered that most human work was never original either. Both are right, and the lesson is the same. The first idea that arrives is usually the shelf's idea, whoever you are. Emma Coats's Pixar story rules put it in rule 12: discount the first thing that comes to mind, and the second, third, fourth and fifth, and surprise yourself.

## 2. Readers notice, and they punish it with stars

In 2025, readers of Lena McDonald's romantasy *Darkhollow Academy: Year 2* found a note left in the middle of a love scene in chapter three. The passage had been rewritten "to align more with J. Bree's style," with "more tension, gritty undertones, and raw emotional subtext." 404 Media bought a copy and confirmed the note had since been cut. Futurism reported a run of one-star reviews, one saying the book was written with generative AI "as is clear by the prompt." The Daily Dot found chatbot leftovers in books by K.C. Crowne and Rania Faris the same season.

Read what the McDonald prompt asked for. More tension. Gritty undertones. Raw emotional subtext. Those are the words of someone who can tell a scene isn't working and can't tell what's missing, so she asks for adjectives. You can't type "more tension." You can type a man who sets a pie on her knee and doesn't take his hand back. A weak outline makes the same request in the same words ("tension rises," "the stakes deepen") and hands the author a feeling to produce with nothing to produce it from.

In Book Riot, S. Zainab Williams wrote that readers want "the human touch and unique perspectives." They now meet machine prose under the books too. On AlphaNovel, one review of *The Alpha's Unloved Luna* calls it "a testament to the author's outstanding writing abilities," straight off the Wikipedia list, and the same page carries a "review" that's a generic essay about a children's picture book.

## 3. Where this book's stock phrases come from: the look

Chapters 1 to 70 are fixed, and this file isn't a prose critique. But the page shows something an outliner can use.

Search the manuscript for its stock phrases and they gather in one kind of moment. In Ch 29, the breakfast where he orders her to look at him, his eyes are "hard as polished emeralds in a face carved of unyielding stone," and then "Every fibre of my being screamed" and "My pulse roared in my ears." In Ch 30 come "piercing green eyes that seemed to see right through me" and "After an eternity." In Ch 13, "something faintly unpleasant flickered across his features." Ch 58, 59 and 64 each have a version of eyes never leaving, the exact phrase from the Antislop list. Every one is a beat where two people look at each other and the writer has to say what a look is like.

Now search for the lines a reader remembers, and they gather somewhere else. In Ch 49 the Head Cook "shook out the whip and wiped his palm on his apron. He glanced at the sun," and Rhett watches the post "like a man watching a chore get done." In Ch 64 she finishes the last pie, "balled up the empty paper and shoved it at him." In Ch 65, "the sting of her split scabs didn't stop her." In Ch 57 Ronan hears the gist of her taunt through two doors: the King "went through girls like dirty washing," a laundry girl's picture in a laundry girl's mouth. Every one is a beat where somebody does something with an object or a body.

So the beat you hand the author decides which kind of sentence they'll reach for. "Their eyes lock across the corridor" hands them a look, and a look pulls in emeralds and stone. "She lays her wrapped hand on the bolt and his voice comes through it into her palm" hands them an act and a thing, and that's how Ch 70 actually reads.

Chuck Palahniuk's essay on "thought verbs" makes the same point from the other side. He bans knows, wants, realizes, remembers, loves and the rest for six months, because "Thinking is abstract," and asks for the physical evidence instead. His example: don't say a girl likes a boy; show her heel mark on his locker, where she keeps leaning between classes. The author's rule against labelled emotions is the same rule. An outline that says "Maren realises she loves him" has broken it before the author writes a word.

Orwell called lazy prose "phrases tacked together like the sections of a prefabricated hen-house," and his first rule is never to use a figure of speech you're used to seeing in print. The famous Chekhov line about the moon and the glint on broken glass isn't his wording, I checked. Wikipedia's "show, don't tell" article treats it as a paraphrase of a letter to his brother that builds a moonlit night from a glint off a broken bottle and the shadow of a dog. The real advice beats the poster: pick the two details only this night has.

## 4. Slop at the outline level

Sentence slop is easy to see. Plot slop does more damage, because it hides inside a competent paragraph.

**The factory template.** Rest of World (2022) and The Spinoff (2024) reported how the werewolf serials on the big apps get made. Editors adapt outlines from popular stories and pay writers overseas for "similar variations." One Dreame contract required 50,000 words a month and a chapter a day, for 6 to 8 percent of profits. GoodNovel's own guide recommends a "single dominant male Alpha" ruling over enslaved commoners, and The Spinoff found the heroine usually called Luna or Thea. The furniture of this shelf (cursed king, scheming beta, prophecy, cold mother, cruel sister) was built from a template on a quota, and the diagnosis found this book reads generic in exactly the scenes that lean on it. Pick up one of those pieces and do the expected thing with it, and you're copying the factory.

**The idiot plot and the misunderstanding.** Damon Knight credited James Blish with the term "idiot plot," and Roger Ebert made it famous: a plot that only keeps moving because everyone in it behaves like a fool. Its romance form is the misunderstanding one honest sentence would end. In a Goodreads thread on tired tropes, a reader named Scarlet said she can't stand it when "the plot is all based on a misunderstanding," and another added that the characters never just ask each other. Isabelle Popp defends the trope in Book Riot, and her defence is the useful part. It works when the misunderstanding is one contained event and the truth changes less than the characters feared. It fails when one fact is withheld and nobody checks the one detail that would settle it. The sources split on the trope and agree on the test.

**The rescue that arrives on time.** Pixar's rule 19: coincidences that get characters into trouble are great, and coincidences that get them out are cheating. In this genre the cheat has local forms: the bond that brings him running whenever she's hurt, the talk overheard through a door, the guard who walks in at the right second. The rulebook marks all of them down, and the author ruled out the bond version after Ch 40.

**A chorus with one voice.** On Goodreads, reviewer Adwoa faulted *The Mistake and the Lycan King*, a dark Lycan captive romance close to this one, for "a lack of distinctive, compelling voice amongst the characters" and for women "all written to antagonize her." The diagnosis found the same here: sow, hog, cow, blob of lard, from a dozen mouths at one volume. When every enemy says the same kind of thing, the reader hears the author's thumb on the scale, and the insults stop landing.

**The claim as a stand-in.** A reviewer of Jessica Hall's *Alpha's Regret* summed up the hero's whole approach as "you are mine and I own you," and another said he "acts like a 14 year old with an impulse control problem." The growled "Mine," the darkening eyes, the snarl at another man: that's where a lazy outline parks a romantic beat it hasn't thought of yet. This book has mostly stayed clear. The nearest line is in Ronan's head in Ch 66, "not until she knew who she belonged to," and it works because it has a condition in it.

**More threads than payoffs.** On AlphaNovel, readers of *Alpha King's Lost Luna* said the middle "started dragging" and that there was "no closure for several of the main side characters." A Goodreads reviewer called *Alpha's Regret* "way too long and too many characters and subplots," and another said that once the secrets were all out, it got boring. That's the other half of the warning. A book running on secrets alone has nothing left once they're spent, so each reveal has to cost somebody something between the two leads.

**The machine's plot habits.** Model writing likes to restate its point at the end, the Wikipedia guide notes. In fiction that becomes characters explaining their feelings, villains explaining the plan, and chapters closing on a summary of their own meaning ("and I knew nothing would ever be the same"). It's one habit, a writer who doesn't trust the scene to have done its work, and the rulebook bans all three.

**Convergence.** This matters most in a tournament. Anil Doshi and Oliver Hauser published a study in *Science Advances* in 2024 in which 293 people wrote eight-sentence stories, some with story ideas from GPT-4, and 600 readers judged them. The stories with AI ideas scored higher for novelty, and the least creative writers gained most. They were also more alike: closeness to the average story in their group rose by about 10.7 percent of the scale. Each writer did better while the field narrowed, which the authors call a social dilemma. Thirty-two outlines built from the same files face the same pull, whoever writes them, and a good outline that matches six others loses to a good one that stands alone.

## 5. The outline is prose too, and it can be slop

The author threw out their own pencilled plan as much for how it read as for what it said. It was "annoying to read," and written "like checklists." Take that literally. A plan is a piece of writing, and Wikipedia's signs fit a bad plan uncomfortably well: bold labels doing the work inside a chapter ("Romance beat:", "Hook:"), lists of three, "pivotal," "sets the stage for," "deepens their bond," trailing "-ing" phrases that pretend to explain ("highlighting her growth"), and a last line restating what the section just said.

Here's a scene that's already written, Ch 63 to 64, told two ways.

The slop version: *"Ronan takes Maren on a pivotal drive to a nearby Lycan town, where a tender moment over food reveals his softer side, highlighting their evolving dynamic and setting the stage for the confrontation to come."*

The human version: *"He parks in shadow outside the one place in that black valley town still lit, goes in barefoot with his shirt hanging open, and drops a hot paper bundle of meat pies in her lap. 'Eat.' She won't, because her hunger is the only weapon she has on him, until her stomach growls loud enough that he winces. He asks if she's trying to punish him. Then he takes the bundle off her and holds a pie to her lips, and she snatches it out of his hand with her wrapped fingers and eats like she's doing it to spite him. When she asks with her mouth full how long before he takes her back, he tells her not to talk with her mouth full. That's the line readers will screenshot."*

The second is longer and has no abstract word in it. Every clause is something the author can write, and it ends on an opinion. "In depth, with no verbose or vague words" sounds like a contradiction until you try it. Depth comes from the specific thing. A vague word usually sits where the writer hasn't found the specific thing yet. If you can't name the object, the line and the act, the beat isn't ready, and the vague word is covering for it.

## 6. What gives a book its own feel

Readers pay for difference and say so. AlphaNovel readers praised *The Broken Luna* as "a bit different from all the other wolf stories." The praise for *The Mistake and the Lycan King* goes to a heroine who "uses a mixture of wits and compassion to help her survive" and to banter that was "hilarious." I found nobody praising a book for its pack hierarchy.

The reliable source of that difference is detail one world could produce and no other. Springhole's guide to werewolf clichés names the agonising change with cracking bones as one shifter books repeat endlessly. This book's rule cuts it: the rot causes no pain, ever, and the change is total and instant. A reader who has sat through the bone-cracking change a hundred times hasn't seen this one.

The list of what's only here is long, and the dossier has it: lye that feels pleasant before it eats her, fingertips that can't tell linen from skin, a bond that bills him for her hunger, boots in a passage of slippers, a yard that counts the lashes out loud, a bolt on the outside of a metal door, a King who drives his own pale car with glowing dials. And the book is funny when it lets itself be: "Reeks of him in here." Slop is almost never funny, because a joke has to come from one particular person in one particular spot.

The rulebook's test is the name swap: if you could change the names and the beat would fit any werewolf book, it's furniture. I'd add a harder one. Ask whether the beat could happen only here, with this bolt, these hands, this bond, this kitchen. If it could happen anywhere, it'll read like everywhere.

## 7. An outline-level checklist

Run this on a finished draft written in prose. Don't build the outline in its shape; that's the mistake the author already rejected.

1. For each chapter, can you name the object, the line and the physical act at its centre? If a chapter runs on a look, a feeling or a realisation, find the act.
2. Did you throw out your first idea for each chapter's turn? Write the obvious version down and ask what thirty-one other outlines will do at this point.
3. Could this beat sit in any werewolf book with the names changed? Could it happen only here?
4. Is every event caused by somebody wanting something, or did it arrive because it was next, by coincidence, or because someone overheard?
5. If something saves her, was it on the page before she needed it?
6. Is there a misunderstanding one sentence would end? Name the reason the sentence can't be said in this chapter. If you can't, have it said.
7. Does anyone explain her feelings, or a villain his plan, in a full speech? Cut it to what that person would actually say, to whom, and for what.
8. Is any romantic beat carried by "Mine," a growl or darkening eyes? What does he do instead?
9. Does a power, a prophecy or the bond solve something a choice should solve?
10. Does each new mystery replace an old one? Is any mystery answered with another?
11. Does every insult come from a mouth with its own reason and target, and does it cost the speaker?
12. Do the chapter endings differ in kind? Does any end on a summary of what it meant?
13. Search the draft for pivotal, palpable, visceral, electric, crackles, tapestry, testament, underscores, highlighting, sets the stage, deepens, "everything changes" and "not just X but Y." Each hit marks a place where the specific thing is missing.
14. Is anything in it funny?
15. Read one chapter section aloud. Does it sound like a person telling a friend about a book they love?

## What this means for Chapters 71–75 of this book

1. **Expect the obvious run to turn up again and again.** It goes roughly: the moon night told straight, the morning after, Nadia brought out, Ilsa caught with the bar, sex by Ch 75. Any of those beats might be right, but Doshi and Hauser show that outlines from the same material converge. Run Pixar's rule 12 on every chapter's turn, and put at least one move in your five chapters that you believe nobody else will make, with a cause you can point to on the page.

2. **Ch 71 is the biggest slop trap in the book.** The beast has been promised since Ch 17, and the likeliest Ch 71 is a monster film: the roar, the splintering door, the scream. This book's rules give you better material. The change is total and instant, with no pain and no half stage. He stands upright and wolf-faced. In Ch 4 the beast nuzzled her instead of killing her. The objects are just as particular: an ordinary bolt anyone outside can draw, no bar, Brida's bare feet beside Maren's, the press, the copper kettle, Nadia told to stay down in the corner. Build whatever happens at that door out of those things and the chapter can't read like anyone else's.

3. **Plan heat around hands and accounts, never looks.** Her fingertips are numb, her nails lifted, one palm has a dead patch, and he remembers "soft hands stroking my fur" from a night when they were still soft (Ch 5). The bond bills his body for what hers feels. A heat beat built from those can't be lifted into another book. One built from eyes darkening across a room will come out as emeralds and stone.

4. **Don't import "Mine."** The book has mostly done without it. Possession here is bookkeeping in the body: her hunger in his gut, her full belly quieting the red. If he claims her in these chapters, make it something he does or says that costs him, in the clipped voice he already has. "I took nothing" (Ch 70) is the model: three words, a plain fact, and the one real answer he's given her so far.

5. **The Nadia misunderstanding is about to fail Popp's test.** It has lasted on his pride and on Nadia being the only other witness. Once Nadia is out of that room alive, the truth sits in a second mouth, and she has reason to use it ("You know. You fucking know what this is?"). An outline that keeps Maren believing he had Nadia past the point where Nadia can talk turns the book into an idiot plot. Either it comes out in these five chapters, or the outline names the specific reason nobody can say it.

6. **Retire the chorus.** If Nadia comes out hating Maren, she has something sharper than "cow" to say now, because Maren watched her walk to that door and said nothing (Ch 68). One line about what Maren did will hit harder than ten about her body. Keep each enemy's mouth its own: Liv goes after class and wolf blood by ruling, and Tove doesn't jab at all.

7. **No rescue by timing.** Brida's bare feet work in Ch 70 because she heard Maren's door and came, and the page showed it. Every save in Ch 71 to 75 needs that footing: whatever saves her is there first, and someone chooses to use it. The same goes for Maren's strength. If it matters, she uses it on purpose and it costs her.

8. **End on a thing or a line, and change the kind each time.** Ch 69 ended on Nadia's yell cut off mid-word, and Ch 70 on Brida heading off to find something heavy for a door with no bar. Five more endings on a sound through a door would be the repeated hook the rulebook penalises. A summary ending ("nothing would ever be the same") is out entirely.

9. **Reach for what only this world has.** The world past the wall is the least spent asset in the book: the road where the truck was hit, the valley town with one place still lit, the car he drives himself. A run that never leaves the King's corridor is one more alpha-in-a-castle book.

10. **Write the outline the way the human version in section 5 reads.** Prose paragraphs, no labels or bold inside chapters, no "pivotal" or "highlighting," and at least one opinion per chapter you'd defend to the author's face. The author rejected their own plan for how it read. A correct outline written like a checklist will lose to a correct outline written by someone who sounds like they love this book.
