# School brief 2: Romance craft

*The arc, the cycle of tension and release, why "will they or won't they" dies, how to keep two people apart honestly, and where Maren and Ronan stand at the end of Chapter 70.*

A romance plot isn't a list of things that happen to two people. It's a list of changes in what they are to each other. A good chapter leaves the couple somewhere different with each other than where it found them, and the change comes from one of them wanting something from the other and paying for it. Readers will wait through almost any delay if they believe the reason for it. They leave when they stop believing it.

That's the brief. Files 3, 5 and 6 cover heat, secrets and cause and effect, so I point to them rather than repeat them.

---

## 1. The arc is a chain of changes, not a chain of events

Romance writers mostly work from two maps. Pamela Regis, a scholar, says every romance has eight elements: the society, the meeting, the barrier, the attraction, the declaration, the point of ritual death, the recognition and the betrothal. Priscilla Oliveras, teaching Regis to working writers, adds three notes that matter here: the elements have no fixed order, the declaration can come at a different time for each lover, and the barrier and the attraction together make the push and pull. One drags them apart, the other drags them back, and the story runs on the strain.

Gwen Hayes, a romance editor, breaks the same ground into twenty beats in four phases. In the setup they meet, find a reason it can't work, and get stuck together anyway ("adhesion"). In falling in love, desire deepens until one of them thinks *maybe this could work*, and the phase ends on the Midpoint of Love, where the connection peaks before the forced separation. Then retreating: doubt, pulling back, shields up, the break. Then fighting for love: the dark night, the wake-up, the grand gesture, proof that it's mutual.

Neither map lists a single event. "They go to a ball" is on neither. Every item names a state of the relationship, which is why a plan written as "Ch 71 The Press, Ch 72 The Beast" reads dead: nothing in those nouns says what the two people are to each other at the end.

Both maps also agree that the middle is a commitment, not a delay. Jami Gold, who teaches romance structure, calls the midpoint a commitment point: a first kiss, first sex or first declaration. It doesn't need witnesses in the story, because the reader can be the witness, and it can look like a sacrifice rather than a confession. After it the tension doesn't drop. It changes subject, from *will they?* to *can this survive what they are?*

**Darcy's first proposal** is the model. Book Riot's scene breakdown puts it at the novel's turning point near halfway and argues the insult is what makes it work. Darcy tells Elizabeth he loves her against his will and against her family's station, in one speech. She refuses him and says why. Then he writes the letter. Nothing went slack when he said he loved her. It got tighter, because now both of them know, and both are wrong about something.

So don't hold back a declaration to protect the tension. Spend it, and let it change the question. And look at Ronan. A man who calls her "the fat one" in his head and wants her anyway is a Darcy proposal waiting to happen. When he declares in his own voice, there will be an insult inside it. That isn't a flaw to fix. It's the scene.

## 2. Attraction: specific, and visible from both sides

Oliveras glosses Regis's attraction as the scenes that show why *this* couple has to be together. Not "he's hot." The particular thing about her that gets to him.

**Jennifer Crusie's *Bet Me*** won the RITA in 2005 and is still the book people name for a plus-size heroine who isn't fixed. Min, as one Smut Report reviewer puts it, ties her worth to her weight. Cal teaches her to cook and tells her butter and carbs are fine, and doughnuts run through the book; the Smut Report reviewers single out the kissing over doughnuts as its hottest moments. Food is how he wants her and how the book argues her body is right. Readers split on it: the Romance Wiki entry notes some find the food focus too much while others credit the book for taking size on at all, and in a 2022 joint review one Smut Report reviewer was shocked by the fat-shaming in the book's world while all three still recommended it. What lasts is the want aimed at the exact thing the world mocks.

**Ali Hazelwood's *Bride*** shows the other half. Smart Bitches gave it a B+ and singled out that the reader never doubts Lowe wants Misery, even while she does, and that his short chapter-opening glimpses add tension. The Wordy Habitat's reviewer said the same glimpses show him gone on her long before he admits it. We know, she doesn't, and we read on to watch the gap close. Falon Ballard's rule for slow burns (Writer's Digest) is the one a two-POV book needs: the pining has to come from both of them, in what they do and say.

**This book** has the want on the page now, and it's about her body: "so much of her to grab, and I'd never had my hands full of a woman like this, and I wanted to fucking die on it, but I couldn't admit that, to her or to myself" (Ch 66). Hers is there too ("Fuck, don't stop"). So the reader is ahead, the way *Bride*'s reader is. The difference is that *Bride* closes its gap. Ronan has never said one good word about her body aloud, and every insult in his head (Ch 25, 27, 52, 57) is a debt the want has to pay down. Irony left that long stops being suspense and becomes waiting.

## 3. Resistance: trouble isn't conflict

Kay Keppler, building on Crusie's line that story is characters in conflict with other characters, draws the line every romance plotter needs. Trouble is bickering, or a misunderstanding two people could clear up by talking. Conflict is someone whose goal works against yours. Trouble fills pages; conflict makes plot.

The romance version is the conflict box. Meredith Bond's rule: the hero's goal has to solve the heroine's conflict, and hers has to solve his. In her own book he wants to marry someone he trusts, she won't marry until she finds her father, she needs his help to look, and helping her teaches him he already trusts her. One word tied the two boxes together, trust, and the book followed from it. Anne Gracie (Writer's Digest) puts it as wounds: old hurts, prejudices and secrets make people protect themselves, and attraction pulls against that.

**Kresley Cole's *A Hunger Like No Other*** is this book's nearest relative. Lachlain, a Lykae king come out of long years of torture by vampires, finds his mate is a vampire. The barrier is his own hatred, and the bond won't let him leave. The Under the Covers reviewer (four stars) found him domineering and Emma seemingly powerless early on, and credited her slow growth more than his persistence for the payoff. In a 2016 Goodreads thread, Harlequin Presents readers said their tolerance for cruel alphas has dropped, and that they quit when a heroine keeps wanting a man who mistreats her and never calls him on it. The same readers said what makes him forgivable: he visibly changes, and she pushes back.

**Patricia Briggs's *Cry Wolf*** shows what a fated bond does to the barrier. Charles's wolf chooses Anna within about a day (Book Smugglers), and the book runs on missteps and slow trust afterwards. Smart Bitches gave it an A because the mating is only partial, so the couple must work out what being together means: instant attachment becomes the conflict, not the cure. The bond doesn't remove the barrier. It moves it inside.

**The conflict box for Maren and Ronan**, filled in from the page. He wants an heir before the rot finishes him, and Maren kept alive and in his bed on his terms. He also wants never to be owned: he took Nadia to "prove the fat wolf didn't own me" (Ch 57), and his terms for sex are "not until she knew who she belonged to" (Ch 66). She wants to be wanted as herself and not as "what was left over" (Ch 61), to stand beside him instead of a pace behind (Ch 69), and never to be used and put back, which the breeder rule makes concrete: the son is taken and the mother goes back to the laundry (Ch 54).

Lock them. His goal, an heir on his terms, is her conflict, because that's the breeder rule. Her goal, to be wanted as herself beside him, is his conflict, because wanting her that way means admitting she owns him. The word that ties the boxes is *owned*. He's afraid of being owned by her; she's afraid of being owned by his family. No single sentence unties that, and the book has pulled on it since Ch 57 without anyone saying it aloud.

## 4. Push and pull: tension, release, and the bill

Under every romance cycle is the rhythm Dwight Swain named and Randy Ingermanson teaches: a scene is goal, conflict, disaster; a sequel is reaction, dilemma, decision, and the decision is the next scene's goal. Ingermanson's worked example is a romance subplot, where one event is a scene for one lover and a disaster for the other. In romance, a push is one of them going after something from the other, a pull is the disaster that sends them back, and the sequel ends with someone deciding what to do.

Gracie calls it two steps forward and one back. LaQuette, in a craft essay for Catapult, names what kills it: drama made by throwing grenades at characters instead of setting their goals against each other, and repetition, the soap-opera loop where the reader can call the next turn. And Ballard's last rule is that the payoff has to pay. A release that leaves the couple where they started was a stall.

Near misses run out fast. In a Goodreads thread among romance writers and readers on whether a slow burn can be too slow, one writer thought more than one near miss looks contrived and another put the ceiling at about three. Readers in the thread said they'd finish a book that moved too slowly and skip the next one. On a pay-per-chapter platform, the next book is the next chapter.

**The best cycle in this book** runs Ch 50 to 65, and every link is a want. She stops eating so he'll hurt (Ch 50). It kills him three times in another woman's bed (Ch 58 to 59). He works out it's aimed at him and asks his first real question, "Are you trying to punish me?" (Ch 63). He feeds her by force with pies from the all-night place (Ch 64). Her full belly gives him his first quiet in days (Ch 65). Each turn costs someone: she loses her weapon, he loses the pretence that she can't reach him. **The worst** is Ch 57 to 59, the same disaster three times. The near misses are Ch 60 and 66. The Ch 66 stop is caused (she smells Nadia on him) and right, once. A third would read as the author flinching.

## 5. Why "will they or won't they" dies

The story everyone tells is *Moonlighting*. Maddie and David slept together in "I Am Curious... Maddie," aired March 31, 1987, and per ComicBook.com the show went from ninth that season to twelfth, then forty-ninth in its last. The lesson people drew, the Moonlighting curse, is that once the leads get together the tension is gone.

The sources disagree, and the disagreement teaches more than the curse. Mental Floss lists Bruce Willis's film career, Cybill Shepherd's pregnancy, discord on set that creator Glenn Gordon Caron described, and dialogue arriving an hour before shooting. PopCult's episode guide notes the pregnancy kept Shepherd from sharing scenes with Willis, and that the story sent Maddie home to Chicago, where she married another man. My read of the record: getting them together didn't kill the show. What came after did. The writers parted the couple by distance and a stranger, and the two people the audience came for stopped being in the same room. "Will they" got its answer and nobody asked the next question.

Rebecca Connolly, a romance novelist, makes the writer's case: being together brings different problems, and the useful question is what could tear them apart now. *Cry Wolf* proves it inside the genre.

So "will they or won't they" dies two ways. The reason for *won't* stops being believed (in the slow-burn thread the killer is tension gone cold, attraction noticed over and over with nothing changing), or *they did* arrives with no new question. Both are failures of the reason, not the timing. Ballard's line is the one to pin up: a slow burn doesn't have to mean a slow pace.

## 6. Keeping them apart honestly

File 5 covers the one-sentence test. My question is which separations are honest, and what happens when the honest sentence gets said. Gracie is blunt: avoid the big misunderstanding a simple question would clear up. Meeghan Reads makes the reader's case about third-act breakups: readers don't mind conflict, only conflict that didn't need to happen, and a split that sends the couple back unchanged is padding. Even well-liked books get hit for it. The Wordy Habitat's reviewer enjoyed *Bride* and still called its third-act breakup unnecessary and senseless.

The defense gives you the test. Lady Geeks Media argues a breakup works when the fear behind it was built long before and fails when it comes from nowhere. Tessa Dare asks whether the misunderstanding is a symptom of the conflict or its cause; her characters misread each other because they misread themselves. Her example is *Gone with the Wind*: Scarlett won't call for Rhett because she believes he doesn't love her, and he waits for her call. Neither is stupid. Each silence is the character.

So: if what keeps them apart is *information*, it's trouble and should come out soon. If it's *the lock*, it's honest and can run the length of the book. And when the information does come out, the best version makes the truth worse than the lie, because the truth drags them onto the lock.

Sorted that way, this book's separations look like this. **Honest:** the moon and his fear of killing her (the bar, the order to Brida, the wives who died in his bed, Ch 52, 70), the breeder rule, a crown that won't take a wolf, his fear of being owned, hers of being used. **Honest but going stale:** she thinks he had Nadia. His silence has a reason on the page ("my pride wouldn't let me," Ch 66) and it comes from the lock, so nine chapters in it's still a symptom. But the lie makes him a man who had another woman, and the truth makes him a man whose body answers to a laundry girl's stomach. He'd rather she believed the lie, which is why the truth is the stronger scene: it doesn't end their conflict, it moves it onto *owned*. **Honest but static:** his terms ("not until she begged me," Ch 66) and hers (make him earn her, Ch 67). Crusie, writing on agency, calls this the negative goal: a goal to keep something from happening, which leaves a character unable to move. Both leads now hold negative goals toward each other, and two people both refusing to move is what sideways feels like. **Thin:** Maren hiding the missing bar from Halvard with no reason on the page (Ch 68).

## 7. The black moment, and the gesture that answers it

Regis's point of ritual death is a loss that makes the reader believe the ending is doomed (Oliveras's gloss); Sarah Frantz notes romance writers already call it the dark moment, and LaQuette says it's where the character's whole arc shows.

The best one I know comes from the heroine's own silence. In Chapter 46 of *Pride and Prejudice*, Elizabeth is at the inn at Lambton, Darcy is in the room, and Jane's letter says Lydia has run off with Wickham. Elizabeth knew what Wickham was from Darcy's letter and kept quiet. "When I consider that I might have prevented it! I who knew what he was." Then Austen gives her the realisation at the worst moment: "never had she so honestly felt that she could have loved him, as now, when all love must be vain." The dark moment comes out of her own choice and lands exactly when the love is clearest.

The answer is Hayes's grand gesture, and readers have a sharper word for what they want from a hero who did harm: the grovel. Jen Prokop, co-host of the Fated Mates podcast, wrote the essay readers point to. A grovel is atonement driven by fear with some hope in it, not humiliation, and her rule is that it should match the offense in place, tone and size. Her MacLean Amendment, after Sarah MacLean: "Groveling isn't an apology; it's change at a molecular level." She also warns some behaviour is too toxic for any grovel to fix. In a long Smart Bitches thread readers say the same: they want to *see* the change, and one said of a hero who kept apologising that the more he did it, the less you liked him. The Harlequin Presents readers complained of heroines who forgive before any real confrontation. And this subgenre asks for it by name: when Smart Bitches asked for book-long grovels, readers named Cate C. Wells's werewolf book *The Tyrant Alpha's Rejected Mate*.

**The gesture that costs the right thing.** In *Persuasion*, Wentworth sits at a writing table nearly turned away while Anne tells Captain Harville that women love longest. His pen falls, and she realises he's nearer than she thought. The letter he hands her opens "I can listen no longer in silence" and owns his fault: "Unjust I may have been, weak and resentful I have been, but never inconstant." It works because it costs the thing he has guarded for eight years, his pride, and puts it in her hand where she can refuse it. Nor is it rescue by eavesdropping, which this book's slop rules ban: Anne says it aloud in a room he's in, and the letter is an answer, not a discovery.

**For Ronan, two things follow.** His offenses were spoken and public: "the fat one," the scrubbing, the laundry, Nadia sent to his room with the wing watching. By Prokop's rule, the repair has to be spoken, and at least once it has to happen where someone can see. And his gesture will never be a speech. He talks to her almost only in orders, and the book has already found the register where his orders give something away: "Eat" with the pies (Ch 63), "Next time, use the other one" after the slap (Ch 40), "I took nothing" through the door (Ch 70). His declaration will be an order or a plain fact that costs him, which also keeps clear of the rulebook's ban on characters explaining their feelings.

## 8. Where Maren and Ronan are on the arc right now

Against Regis: the society, the meeting (Ch 3 to 4), the barrier and the attraction are all on the page. The declaration is half done. Hers is of status, "I may have been born a tribute, but I'm your mate now" (Ch 40). His is of fact, the bond named aloud (Ch 30). Neither has declared a feeling. Ritual death, recognition and betrothal haven't started.

Against Hayes: the setup is long finished. He had her scrubbed and sent to the laundry (the first *no way*), and the bond snapping in Ch 27 was the adhesion. Falling in love has run its course: the inkling ("And yet I looked," Ch 14), the deepening (Ch 60, 66), and one real *maybe this could work*, the pies (Ch 63 to 65), the only time they've been fed, teased and quiet together. Then Ch 66 jumped straight to retreat beats: doubt (she smells Nadia), retreat (the shove), shields up ("Bar your door tonight").

**The Midpoint of Love never happened.** They are retreating from a commitment they never made, and that's the cause of the sideways feeling in Ch 67 to 70: the doubt beats fire with nothing yet to doubt. Their terms are mirror images, him waiting for her to beg and her waiting for him to earn it, with Brida's question over both: what if he stops waiting? (Ch 67). Six days have passed for them and seventy chapters for the reader, so the midpoint is overdue for the reader, not the characters. That's good news: two people on a full-moon night, with the bar gone and a girl locked in with a beast, are allowed to move fast.

## What this means for Chapters 71-75 of this book

**1. Land the midpoint in this run, and make it a step neither of them can take back.** Not a third almost. A commitment point in Gold's sense: something one of them does or says that changes what they are to each other, witnessed at least by the reader. It needn't be sex: it could be her choosing to stay on the wrong side of a door, or him handing her the one fact that makes him hers. After it, the book's question should be whether this can survive the breeder rule, the crown and the moon.

**2. Plan the five chapters as moves, and check that none goes sideways.** Before writing a word, write one line per chapter on where the couple stands at its last line compared with its first. If two chapters in a row leave them in the same place, fold one. At least one chapter should move them closer and leave them closer. The pies proved this book does tenderness best when someone is being fed or touched without asking, and that turns the romance more than a fourth fight.

**3. Turn both waits into wants.** "Not until she begged me" and "make him earn me" are negative goals. Give Maren a want aimed at him in every chapter, something she can go after and fail to get: to see what he is tonight, to make him say what happened with Nadia, to stand beside him somewhere he's never let her. Give Ronan one he can only get by giving up some control. Whoever moves first loses face, and that loss is the beat.

**4. Pull on "owned" whenever they're alone.** It's the lock: he took Nadia to prove she didn't own him (Ch 57), his terms are that she knows who she belongs to (Ch 66), and she fears his family owning her son. Her answer to his Ch 66 line should come back in new words, never quoted (the no-repeats rule). The Nadia truth, whoever tells it (files 3, 5 and 6 weigh that), should land on this knot, and since it's worse for his pride than the lie, make him pay it.

**5. Let his declaration come in his own register.** No speech and no "Mine." One order that gives something away, or one plain fact said to her face that costs him, in the line of "Eat" (Ch 63) and "I took nothing" (Ch 70). If it carries an insult inside it the way Darcy's proposal does, better: the insult is what makes a proud man's declaration believable. Write the actual line into the outline. "He admits his feelings" isn't a beat.

**6. If the moon night goes wrong, make it Maren's Lambton.** She knew what the night was and didn't warn Nadia (Ch 68). If Nadia comes out hurt or doesn't come out, that's a point of ritual death caused by the heroine's own silence, landing when her want for him is plainest. Use it to make her choose, not to park her in guilt for three chapters. Whether Nadia lives is your call; either way, the cost of that silence should arrive on the page, between the two of them.

**7. Spend a down payment, keep the grovel.** The full grovel belongs to a later arc, once he's done more to grovel for and she has a real reason to refuse him. Here, give one small, specific act that shows change. When the big repair comes, Prokop's rule says it happens where the offenses did: in front of people, in the places where she walked a pace behind him.

**8. Keep them in the same room.** That's the *Moonlighting* lesson. After whatever commitment lands, don't part them by distance or a third person for more than a chapter. If the plot needs them separated, use the door, the bond and the moon, the things that make each feel the other on the far side.

**9. Make every release pay, and say what it bought.** Each scene between them should read as want, resistance, result, decision: she goes after something from him, he or the palace resists, it lands worse or different than she meant, and her decision opens the next chapter. A chapter that's all decision and no attempt (Ch 62 asking "Where are we going?" four ways) is half a chapter.
