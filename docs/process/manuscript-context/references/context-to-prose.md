# Context to Prose — Translating the Brief Into the Opening

## The Gap Between Knowing and Writing

Running the context protocol gives you knowledge. Knowledge is not prose. The gap between having accurate information about where a character is and writing a chapter that feels like that character is where all the work happens.

This reference covers how to make that translation.

---

## What the Context Brief Should Produce

After running the protocol, you should be able to answer these questions without searching again:

1. What does the POV character want in this scene, right now, today — not in general but specifically?
2. What are they afraid of finding out, or being seen doing, or having to admit?
3. What just happened that this scene is a response to?
4. Who else is in this scene and what do they want from the POV character?
5. What is the scene's surface — what it appears to be about?
6. What is the scene's engine — what it is actually about underneath?

If you cannot answer all six, search more.

---

## Surface vs. Engine

Every strong scene has two layers.

The **surface** is what the characters are doing and saying. Two characters in a room. A conversation. An event. This is what the scene describes.

The **engine** is what the scene is actually about. The unspoken thing underneath the surface action. The fear or want or grief that drives every choice the POV character makes, even when they aren't aware of it.

The context protocol is what reveals the engine. Without it, you can write the surface. The scene will be technically functional and emotionally empty.

**Example:**

Surface: Mina is in her room after witnessing Sera break Irina with the recording crystal.

Engine: Mina is in the presence of an echo she cannot let herself name — the old Luna's psychological destruction of her mother is in the architecture of what Sera just did, and Mina handed Sera the instrument for it, and the question she cannot face is whether the person she chose as family is capable of becoming the thing that destroyed the only family she had.

The surface is a woman alone in a room at night. The engine is everything. A chapter that only writes the surface is the chapter that "achieves nothing" and "just rehashes." A chapter that writes from the engine — without announcing it, without stating it, letting it operate in what Mina notices and doesn't notice, what she straightens and what she avoids looking at — is the chapter that the reader feels.

---

## How to Open Once You Know the Engine

This reference governs the choice of what the opening is actually about.

Once you know the engine, the opening should place the reader inside it without announcing it. The first sentence should contain the engine in disguised form — as a specific observation, a blunt statement, an absence, a wrong detail.

For the Mina chapter after the recording crystal:

The engine is the echo — Sera doing to Irina what the old Luna did to Mina's mother, and Mina's complicity in it, and the question of whether she is watching history repeat or something worse.

An opening that announces this: "She couldn't stop thinking about her mother." — Wrong. Named. Analytical.

An opening that contains it: "She'd put the crystal back in the trunk herself. She didn't know why that was the thing she kept returning to." — Right. Specific. The crystal going back in the trunk is the action of a person managing something they don't want to look at directly. The reader feels the engine without being told what it is.

---

## When the Scene Has Multiple Characters

Pull the engine for every character in the scene, not just the POV character.

Every character in a scene wants something and fears something. When those wants and fears are in tension with each other — when what character A needs from character B is exactly what character B cannot give right now — that tension is where the scene lives.

If the wants and fears don't create tension, the scene has no engine. In that case, either the scene shouldn't exist, or the character wants and fears haven't been pulled correctly from the manuscript.

---

## The Check Before Writing

Once you have the context brief and have identified the surface and the engine, ask one final question before starting:

**If this chapter were removed from the manuscript entirely, what would be lost?**

If the answer is "nothing important" — if the scene is just characters existing without anything changing or being revealed or being at stake — don't write it. Find the scene that should exist instead and write that.

If the answer names something specific — a shift in a relationship, a revelation, a decision that changes what comes next, a moment where the reader sees something the POV character can't see — then write toward that thing. That is what the chapter is for.
